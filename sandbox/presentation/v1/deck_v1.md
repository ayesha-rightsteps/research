## Slide 2 — Two decisions must be made together: who flies where, and how to stay safe

One Stage 3 layout from our environment: 8 drones, 20 obstacle discs, 500 × 500 world
- **Assignment** — Which drone flies to which target? The targets move, so the answer changes at every step.

**Say:** This picture is our simulated world, taken straight from the environment code. The blue circles are drones, the orange diamonds are their targets (the number tells you which drone a target belongs to), and the grey discs are obstacles. The dashed lines are the assignment.

## Slide 5 — One framework: assignment and conflict graph feed MAPPO; PAH balances mission against safety

*Section: Problem and method*

**On slide:**

- [box] 2D world / moving targets, / obstacle discs
- [box] Hungarian assignment / every step → assigned target
- [box] Conflict graph / drones on a collision course
- [box] 24-ray LiDAR / distance to obstacles
- [box] Observation / per drone / (50 numbers)
- [box] Shared actor / (MAPPO)
- [box] Action / 2D velocity
**TRAINING ONLY**
- [box] Centralised critic / two value heads: / mission and safety
- [box] Advantages / A_mission and A_safety
- [box] PAH / τ, d_target, / n_conflict → α
- [box] Actor update / α · A_mission + / (1 − α) · A_safety
trains the actor

**Say:** Read this diagram from left to right along the top. The world gives each drone three kinds of information: its assigned target from the Hungarian algorithm, which neighbours are on a collision course from the conflict graph, and the distance to obstacles from 24 LiDAR rays. Together that is 50 numbers per drone. One shared actor turns them into a velocity command.
The bottom band is only used during training. The critic estimates two values, one for the mission reward and one for the safety reward, and from them we get two advantages. PAH looks at the situation and produces a weight alpha, and the actor is updated with alpha times the mission advantage plus one minus alpha times the safety advantage.
A useful consequence: PAH shapes the training signal, but it is not used when the drones fly. So it adds no run-time cost. I will show the timing later.

## Slide 6 — PAH turns the situation into a mission-vs-safety weight α

*Section: Problem and method*

**On slide:**

- [box] τ  time to the nearest collision (drone or obstacle, capped at 3 steps)
- [box] d_target  distance to the assigned target
- [box] n_conflict  number of drones on a collision course
- [box] PAH / small network / 3 → 32 → 1
- [box] risk p / predicted danger / from 0 to 1
- [box] α = 0.9 − 0.8 · p / weight on the mission
High risk → α near **0.1**: safety counts more.  Low risk → α near **0.9**: the mission counts more.
> Fixed-weight MAPPO: α = 0.5 always. Hand-coded τ-rule MAPPO: α = 0.1 + 0.8 · τ / 3, nothing learned.
> PAH learns the risk from the safety cost that actually followed, in the same training loop. No formula is copied.

**Say:** PAH is deliberately tiny: three inputs, one hidden layer of 32 units, one output. The three inputs are exactly the ones in the approved synopsis: time to the nearest collision, distance to the target, and the number of drones on a collision course. The time-to-collision now also counts obstacles, which I will explain in the redesign.
The network predicts a risk between zero and one, and alpha is 0.9 minus 0.8 times that risk. So when danger is close, alpha is near 0.1 and safety dominates the learning signal; when nothing is near, alpha is near 0.9 and the mission dominates.
Two comparison methods use the same agent but a different alpha: a fixed 0.5, and a hand-written rule based only on time to collision. The point of the thesis is whether a learned alpha beats these.
If asked whether this is just a learned version of the hand-coded rule: it has the same inputs, but it learns from the safety cost that actually followed, and it turns towards safety earlier than the rule (I show that on the alpha slide). At Stage 3 it beats the rule by 11 points; at Stage 2 it does not clearly beat it.

## Slide 8 — Redesign: four changes, one environment bug fixed, settings tuned once and frozen

*Section: How we got here*

**On slide:**

- **1  Two value heads** — Separate mission and safety critics, so the safety advantage becomes a real safety signal. Because: in version 3 the mixed signal was 95 to 100 % the same as Plain MAPPO's.
- **2  Obstacle-aware τ** — PAH's time-to-collision now includes obstacles. Its three inputs are unchanged. Because: PAH could not see the main danger.
- **3  24-ray LiDAR** — Replaces the four axis-aligned distances of version 3. Because: obstacles caused 94 to 100 % of collisions.
- **4  Sized obstacles** — Discs of radius 12: 10 at Stage 2, 20 at Stage 3 (points before). Because: point obstacles were far easier than the base papers' physical ones.
- **Bug found and fixed** — Targets could push a waiting drone into an obstacle. A hand-written LiDAR rule then reached 82 % with no obstacle collisions (dense Stage 2 setting): the task is solvable.
- **Tuned once, then frozen** — Reward shaping and obstacle density were tuned on Plain MAPPO only, on separate seeds, then fixed for every method.

**Say:** Four changes, each one answering a diagnostic finding. The two value heads make the safety advantage a real safety signal. Counting obstacles in the time-to-collision lets PAH see the main danger. The 24-ray LiDAR gives much better obstacle sensing than four directions. And the obstacles are now discs with a size, not points.
On the right: along the way we found a bug in the environment. Targets bounced off obstacles too close, so a drone waiting on its target could be dragged into one. We fixed it, and checked that the task is solvable by writing a simple hand-coded LiDAR rule, which reached 82 percent with no obstacle collisions in the dense setting. That tells us the task itself is fine, and any remaining difficulty is a learning problem.
Last, the fairness rule: we tuned reward shaping and obstacle density once, on the plain baseline only, using separate seeds, and then froze them for every method. So no method got special tuning.
Please note: the supervisor's agreement to these changes is not written anywhere in the repository, so confirm it before presenting if you want to say it.

## Slide 9 — The first PAH was biased against safety; we found the cause and fixed it once

*Section: How we got here*

**On slide:**

- **1  The synopsis design** — PAH is trained through the policy gradient, together with the actor.
- **2  What it learned** — In danger, α was pushed towards the mission: +0.200, its maximum, at τ < 1.
- **3  Why** — The gradient of α·A_mission + (1 − α)·A_safety with respect to α is A_mission − A_safety. In danger A_safety is very negative, so the gradient raises α.
- **4  The fix, made once** — PAH predicts the risk and learns by regression on the safety cost that followed. No gradient from the actor loss. Same inputs, same 3 → 32 → 1 network.
> Measured on the first PAH's final model (Stage 3, seed 42, 100 test episodes): +0.200 for τ < 1 (134 states). The biased version is kept as a finding.
> This is the one deliberate change to PAH compared with the synopsis, which trained it through the policy gradient.

**Say:** This is the part I am proudest of, because it is a negative result handled honestly.
Step one: my first PAH followed the synopsis. It was trained through the same policy-gradient update as the actor.
Step two: when I looked at what it had learned, it did the opposite of its purpose. In danger it pushed alpha as high as allowed, towards the mission.
Step three: the reason is mathematical. The gradient with respect to alpha is the mission advantage minus the safety advantage. In danger the safety advantage is strongly negative, so the gradient says: raise alpha, ignore safety. The model was simply following that gradient.
Step four: I changed the design once. Now PAH predicts the risk and is trained by regression on the safety cost that actually followed, with no gradient from the actor loss. Same inputs, same tiny network.
I found this by looking at the mechanism, not by tuning performance. The blue box gives the measurement; the biased version is kept as a finding. The orange box is the one place where PAH differs from the synopsis.
If asked 'did you change PAH after seeing seed 42, and is that tuning on the test set?': the change was driven by the mechanism, tested on synthetic data and a 300-episode smoke run, then frozen before any of the final runs. Without seed 42 the main result is +7.9, almost unchanged, and on seed 42 itself the new PAH is slightly below the hand-coded rule (79.5 against 82.0 percent), so the design was not shaped to win that seed.

**Say:** A committee member who has read the synopsis may ask about differences, so I would rather list them myself.

## Appendix A13 · RMAPPO: what happened

*Section: Appendix*

**On slide:**

- **What we observed** — Recurrent MAPPO under the shared settings: 0.0 % success on all 6 seeds at Stage 3 (collision 86.3 %, timeout 13.7 %). Stage 2 was not run. The policy's random noise collapsed early. Seed 45: log-std reached its lower limit of −4.0 by episode 2,000 and entropy fell from 1.9 to −5.2; the other seeds ended between −2.9 and −3.5. Other methods on the same stage (seed 45) ended between about −0.9 and 0.
- **What we checked and what we did not** — Replaying the stored sequences gives the stored log-probabilities (largest difference 9.5e-07), so the hidden state handling is correct; the speed-up of the code changed weights by less than 1e-6. Not tested: a larger batch. The shared 64-step minibatch gives only 4 sequences per minibatch; the original RMAPPO uses larger batches. Therefore RMAPPO is reported here with the reason, not as a performance number.

**Say:** Use this if asked why RMAPPO is not in the comparison. We found no implementation bug; the likely cause is the small minibatch of recurrent sequences, but that was not tested. A re-run with its original large-batch setting would take about one night.
